  <summary><b>1. Can you describe a time when you had to learn something new quickly? How did you approach it?</b></summary>
  <h4>Situation</h4>
<p>"When I started my graduate research assistantship at UNT, I was assigned to build an AI-powered query system for Friction Stir Additive Manufacturing research — FSAM. I had a computer science background but FSAM is a materials science field I had never encountered before."</P>

<h4>Task</h4>
<p>"My task was to build a system that could answer natural language questions about FSAM research. But to do that effectively, I first needed to understand the domain — the terminology, the research questions, what mattered to scientists in that field."</P>

<h4>Action</h4>
<p>"I took a three-step approach. First, I connected directly with the materials science department — I met with Professor Mishra and his students to understand what questions they actually needed answered and what data existed. Second, I read research papers in the field to understand the core concepts — alloy types, process parameters, mechanical properties. Third, I researched online resources and existing FSAM datasets to understand the data structure before building the ingestion pipeline."</P>

<h4>Result</h4>
<p>"Within a few weeks I had enough domain knowledge to design the right system architecture — a hybrid SQL plus vector retrieval approach that matched how scientists actually ask questions. The final system successfully queried across 57 peer-reviewed papers and reduced literature review time from days to seconds for the research team."</p>
  
<h4> Conclusion </h4>
<p>"That experience taught me that when learning something new, going directly to the experts and combining that with self-study is always faster than trying to figure it out alone."</P>

  <summary><b>5. How do you ensure clear communication when working on a remote team? 
</b></summary>

  <h4>Situation</h4>
  <p>During my Mood Tracking app project I worked with a remote team where we couldn't always meet in person or communicate in real time.</p>

  <h4>Task</h4>
  <p>We needed to make sure everyone knew what was being worked on, what was done, and what was blocked — without constant meetings or messages.</p>

  <h4>Action</h4>
  <p>We used a few key practices. First, we tracked all tasks in Jira — every feature, bug, and task had a ticket with a clear description, assigned owner, and status. That way anyone could see progress without asking. Second, we held brief daily standups — quick check-ins where each person shared what they did, what they're doing, and if anything was blocking them. Third, for code we used Git with clear, descriptive commit messages so teammates could understand changes without explanation. And whenever I finished a task I updated the ticket status immediately rather than waiting — so the team always had accurate information</p>

  <h4>Result</h4>
  <p>The team stayed aligned throughout the project with minimal confusion. We avoided duplicate work and caught blockers early because everything was visible in one place</p>

  <h4>Conclusion</h4>
  <p>I believe clear communication on a remote team comes down to one principle — never make your teammates guess. Keep everything visible, update statuses proactively, and over-communicate rather than under-communicate</p>
</details>

  <summary><b>6. Describe a time you had a disagreement with a teammate. How did you resolve it? 
</b></summary>

  <h4>Situation</h4>
  <p>During my Mood Tracking app project, our team was building out the journal section and creating the feature list together.</p>

  <h4>Task</h4>
  <p>I wanted to add a specific feature to the journal section but my teammate pushed back saying we didn't have enough time to implement it before the deadline</p>

  <h4>Action</h4>
  <p>Instead of insisting or getting frustrated I first listened to understand their concern — the time constraint was real and valid. Then I presented my case clearly — I explained why I felt the feature added value for the user experience and estimated how long it would actually take to build. We had an open conversation about priorities. In the end we agreed on a middle ground — we implemented a simplified version of the feature that covered the core functionality without taking too much time. We documented the full version as a future enhancement</p>

  <h4>Result</h4>
  <p>We shipped on time and the simplified version of the feature was still valuable. More importantly the team stayed aligned and there was no tension going forward. I learned that disagreements are easier to resolve when you listen first and focus on the goal — delivering a good product — rather than winning the argument.</p>

  <h4>Conclusion</h4>
  <p>I think healthy disagreement is actually good for a team — it means people care about the outcome. The key is keeping the conversation focused on the goal, not on being right</p>
</details>

  <p>The customer was satisfied with how it was handled. After that experience I made sure to learn the full refund and billing process properly so I would never be in that situation again. It taught me that mistakes happen — what matters is how quickly and honestly you respond to them</p>
